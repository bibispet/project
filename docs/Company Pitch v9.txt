The Core Problem: Families Losing Control of Their Identity and Heritage

In a world of surveillance and cultural blur, families lack a secure, owned record of who they are



Official identities are locked in government registries and platforms families don't control. Schools, social media, data brokers aggregate profiles from birth certificates, enrollment forms, and online activities, often without consent or transparency, leading to fragmented and externally dictated narratives.

Real family lore (migrations, languages, faith, house rules, spiritual traditions, etc) fragments or vanishes without a canonical vault, as physical artifacts degrade and digital files get lost - cultural contexts that define heritage, and multi-generational stories, are slowly ERASED.

Rising global immigration (304 million international migrants in 2024, representing 3.7% of the world's population) accelerates cultural "blur," making preservation urgent as families navigate mixed languages, dispersed relatives, and diluted traditions across borders.



Two Systemic Threats to Family Data

History either erodes quietly or gets exploited aggressively



Forgetting: Photos, stories, and documents scatter across obsolete drives, apps, and physical media, with no unified source of truth (determined by the user/family, and if not - then who else?). Data has to survive relocations, device upgrades, or account closures, resulting in permanent gaps that deprive future generations of their roots.

Harvesting: Platforms mine childhood data for ads and AI training (e.g. facial recognition software); data brokers compile and sell profiles on kids without family oversight, turning personal milestones into commodities while exposing vulnerabilities like location data or routines to unauthorized access.

Balance: We protect both heritage (preserving oral histories, migrations, and faith milestones) and future identity (ensuring sovereign control over data to prevent external rewriting or exploitation), addressing immediate emotional losses and long-term privacy risks.



Forgetting: Fragmented and Fragile Archives

We have centralised systems for people and their money - but no system for their heritage



Physical media (albums, letters, SIM cards) decays over time due to environmental factors or obsolescence, while digital trails split across iCloud, WhatsApp, Instagram, and Gmail, lacking shared structure or automatic backups, making retrieval almost impossible after years.

Immigrant families face amplified risk: Migration and language drift blur heritage fast. Surveys show 40% of families lose key photos or documents in the move, severing the identity link for diaspora communities where oral tradition is key.

Generations inherit incomplete narratives, not full stories. e.g. a child's understanding of ancestral migrations fades without consolidated records, leading to cultural erosion that studies link to higher rates of identity-related mental health challenges in migrant populations.

However, migrants themselves aren’t the only ones suffering from this problem. Large-scale immigration and mixing make it harder for any culture, majority or minority, to keep a clear, record of itself. Without a durable vault, dominant cultures can fade into vague labels, smaller cultures can disappear entirely from lived memory, and future generations inherit fragments instead of a full narrative.



Harvesting: Kids as Data Commodities

Commercial systems own the record; families don’t.



"Sharenting" exposes children's faces, schools, and daily routines to public or semi-public feeds by default, with platforms like Instagram and TikTok optimizing for viral engagement rather than long-term safety, often resulting in unintended data leaks or cyberbullying vulnerabilities.

AI models and brokers build lifelong profiles from scraped data; families have no audit trails or opt-out mechanisms, allowing third parties to monetize sensitive info like health records or locations without accountability.

As more services, jobs, and payments move behind logins, KYC (Know Your Customer) checks, and digital IDs, “opting out” stops being realistic: to work, study, or transact, families MUST feed more data into centralised systems, trading sovereignty for basic participation in the digital economy. Unless your ‘living off the land’ you wont have a choice.

The ‘digital identity’ sector on it’s own is projected to reach $44.20 billion in 2025, driven by privacy fears amid rising data breaches that affect millions annually, highlighting the urgent need for family-controlled alternatives.



The 1%/99% Surveillance Trap

“Rare risks justify blanket control”.



Small harmful activity (e.g. 1% criminal or abusive) triggers 100% logging, ID verification, and monitoring across platforms, extending tools meant for edge cases to everyday users and normalizing pervasive data collection under the guise of protection.

For example, In crypto, illicit activity is estimated at well under 1% of total volume, yet the policy response includes blanket KYC regulation, on-chain surveillance, and delistings/bans of privacy coins, impacting (the 99%), ordinary users who simply want financial privacy.

Privacy tools (encryption, pseudonyms, self-custody) are often flagged as suspicious by algorithms or policies, discouraging their use and pushing families toward less secure, centralized systems that prioritize institutional access over individual rights.

Families trade sovereignty for "safety" e.g. the EU's digital ID rollout by 2025 mandates checks for online access, including biometrics or wallets, potentially gating services like social media or banking for all users regardless of risk level.



Identity Will Follow Money's Path

Centralization accelerates the need for security - history proves it



 In the 20th century, money quietly moved from asset-backed to centrally managed. The 1971 Nixon Shock ended dollar convertibility to gold and centralizing power in the Federal Reserve and banks, which enabled greater surveillance through transaction tracking and monetary policies. Laws like the Bank Secrecy Act and later AML rules normalised large-scale transaction monitoring and reporting by default.

We don’t need ‘conspiracy theories’ to develop a narrative around this project. Believing any ‘conspiracy theories’ like “which president challenged the banks, and what happened to him” isn’t needed to see the pattern. The public record of legislation and regulation is enough.

Present day, central banks and institutions like the IMF are actively exploring central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). Official papers talk about inclusion and efficiency, but a widely adopted CBDC, especially if linked to digital ID, biometrics, and a potential social credit system, will enable OBVIOUS programmable control over who can spend, where, on what, and under which conditions - an unprecedented level of policy enforcement at the individual level. We are most likely being lead into that direction, so the potential demographic LORE targets will expand drastically as people catch on.



Continued (examples)

AML (Anti-Money Laundering)/KYC layers freeze assets globally - e.g. Vietnam's State Bank deactivated over 86 million bank accounts in september 2025 for lacking biometric authentication, restricting transactions and highlighting how compliance mandates can disrupt everyday financial access.

Crypto: Privacy coins face bans in the EU by 2027 under AMLR and MiCA regulations, prohibiting anonymous wallets and tokens like Monero from centralized exchanges, reducing liquidity and pushing privacy-focused users toward decentralized alternatives.


In China, blacklists and social credit systems can already restrict access to flights, trains, and certain jobs, while real-name SIM registration and pervasive cameras link identity, movement, and behaviour into one control layer. Combined with digital currencies and mandatory IDs, this model shows how quickly financial access, mobility, and reputation can be bound together in software.

The best response? Take a look at what people have done in the past - as rails centralise and surveillance expands, edge solutions emerge: first gold in private safes, then crypto in private wallets. The pattern is consistent: when trust and control concentrate, people build vaults they own to store value outside those rails. LORE applies the same logic to identity and heritage.



The Solution
